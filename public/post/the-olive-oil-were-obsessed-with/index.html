I have a confession: I tasted seventeen olive oils in three weeks before I felt ready to write about one. Seventeen bottles, from eleven producers, across four regions. Some were excellent. Some were interesting. One made me stop mid-sip and just sit there, eyes closed, trying to understand what I had just tasted.

That one — the one that stopped me — came from a small family operation on the northern slopes of Velebit, about forty minutes inland from Zadar. The kind of place you don't find by searching. The kind you find because someone's cousin's uncle told you about it.

OPG Vrančić — Buža & Oblica, Harvest 2025

Pour it into a white dish and the first thing you notice is the colour. Not the generic green-gold of supermarket "extra virgin." This is deeper — almost emerald at the edges, with a golden core that catches the light like stained glass. It is the oil equivalent of looking into a clear Adriatic cove on a still morning.

The smell is where it gets personal. Fresh-cut grass, yes — that's the Buža — but underneath it, something unexpected: tomato leaf, green almond, a whisper of artichoke. There's no mistaking it for anything but Dalmatian. The limestone of the terrain announces itself in every note.

And then the taste. The first hit is grassy, almost sweet, the kind of sweetness that comes from fruit picked at exactly the right moment. Then, about three seconds in, the pepper arrives — not aggressive, but confident. A slow burn at the back of the throat that tells you, unmistakably, that this oil was pressed within hours of harvest. High polyphenols. No filtration. Nothing lost.

This is early-harvest oil in the truest sense. The olives were picked in the first week of November, still green, still firm. The oil was cold-extracted the same day. The result is a liquid that has no interest in being subtle. It demands attention.

Who Else Is Doing It Right

I should mention that OPG Vrančić is not alone. A few other producers in the region are working at this level, and they deserve your attention too.

OPG Šimuni, Pag — I mentioned their Buža in Issue #1, but it bears repeating. Pag is not an easy place to grow olives. The wind, the salt, the thin soil. Perhaps that is exactly why their oil is so good. The trees fight for every drop. Their 2025 harvest has a minerality I have not found anywhere else — almost flinty, like the scent of rain on hot stone.

OPG Brkić, Ravni Kotari — A blend of Oblica and Levantinka, with a softer profile. More butter than grass. This is the oil you want on a simple plate of burrata and early tomatoes. It doesn't fight the other ingredients — it joins them.

Eko Maslina, Zemunik — Certified organic, single-variety Oblica. Their 2025 batch is the greenest of the bunch — just-picked grass, almost vegetal, with an intense finish that lingers for a full minute. Not for the faint of heart, but if you want to understand what Oblica can do at its best, this is the bottle.

How to Eat This Oil

The Vrančić oil is not a cooking oil. Do not heat it. Do not dress it on a salad with vinegar that will bully it. This oil deserves to be the main event.

The way I've been using it: a slice of good sourdough — the kind with a crust that fights back — rubbed with a cut clove of garlic, drizzled generously, finished with a pinch of coarse sea salt from Nin. That is the whole recipe. The bread, the garlic, the oil, the salt. Four ingredients. One of them — the oil — doing all the work.

If I am feeling extravagant, I'll add a slice of salted sardine or a soft-boiled egg. But honestly, the bread and the oil are enough. When the oil is this good, everything else is just an interruption.

Seventeen oils. Three weeks. One that stopped me. This is the one.

— Elena
